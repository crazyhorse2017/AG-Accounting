Every year, criminals call, text, and email people pretending to be from the IRS. Their goal is to frighten you into paying money or sharing personal information. A few simple rules make these scams easy to spot.

## How the IRS really contacts you

The IRS usually reaches out first **by regular mail**. If you have a tax problem, you’ll normally get a letter before anyone calls or visits.

## Red flags

Be suspicious of anyone who:

- **Demands immediate payment**, especially by gift card, prepaid debit card, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency
- **Threatens arrest**, deportation, or loss of a driver’s license
- Asks for **credit card or bank numbers over the phone**
- Contacts you **out of the blue by text, email, or social media** about a refund or a bill
- Won’t let you **question or appeal** what you supposedly owe

The real IRS doesn’t do any of these things.

## Text and email scams

Many scam messages promise a refund or a tax credit and include a link. Don’t click it. Fake websites can look just like IRS.gov. To check your account, type **irs.gov** into your browser yourself, or use the IRS Online Account.

## If you get a suspicious call or message

1. Don’t give out any information and don’t pay anything.
2. Hang up or delete the message.
3. If you think you might owe tax, contact the IRS directly using information from IRS.gov, or call us and we’ll help you check.
4. Report the scam using the steps on the IRS’s [tax scams page](https://www.irs.gov/help/tax-scams/recognize-tax-scams-and-fraud).

## Protect yourself before it happens

An **Identity Protection PIN** is a six-digit number from the IRS that stops anyone else from filing a return with your Social Security number. Anyone with an SSN or ITIN who can verify their identity can get one. [Learn how to get an IP PIN](https://www.irs.gov/identity-theft-fraud-scams/get-an-identity-protection-pin).
