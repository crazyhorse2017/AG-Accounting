When you retire, your paycheck is replaced by Social Security, pensions, and withdrawals from savings. Each is taxed differently, and nobody withholds tax unless you ask. Here’s what to watch.

## Bigger deductions after 65

- If you’re 65 or older, you add **$2,050** to your standard deduction if you’re unmarried, or **$1,650** for each spouse 65 or older if married (2026 amounts).
- From 2025 through 2028, people 65 and older may also take a new **$6,000** deduction each, which phases out above $75,000 of modified AGI ($150,000 for joint filers).

## Social Security

Depending on your other income, up to 85% of your benefits may be taxable. See our article [Is my Social Security taxable?](/articles/is-social-security-taxable).

## Pensions and IRA withdrawals

Pensions and traditional IRA or 401(k) withdrawals are generally taxed as ordinary income. A big withdrawal in one year can push you into a higher bracket and make more of your Social Security taxable, so plan large withdrawals carefully.

## Required minimum distributions

You generally must start taking minimum distributions from traditional retirement accounts at **age 73**, or **75** if you were born in 1960 or later. Missing one can bring a steep additional tax. Our [RMD calculator](/calculators/required-minimum-distribution) estimates your amount.

## Give to charity from your IRA

If you’re 70½ or older, you can give directly from your IRA to a qualified charity through a **qualified charitable distribution**. The amount isn’t taxable income and can count toward your RMD. The money must go directly from the IRA to the charity.

## Withholding and estimated taxes

Because no one withholds automatically from most retirement income, many retirees owe at tax time. You can:

- Ask for withholding from Social Security with Form W-4V,
- Ask your pension or IRA provider to withhold, or
- Make quarterly estimated tax payments.

## Ohio and city taxes

Ohio doesn’t tax Social Security benefits, and Ohio cities generally don’t tax retirement income such as pensions and Social Security. Ohio does tax pension and IRA income on the state return, though it offers some credits for retirees.

## Keep an eye on scams

Scammers often target retirees with fake IRS calls. The IRS usually contacts people by mail first, and it never demands payment by gift card.
