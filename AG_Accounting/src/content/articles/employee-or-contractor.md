When you pay someone to work for your business, you need to know whether they’re an **employee** or an **independent contractor**. The answer decides who pays payroll taxes, what forms you file, and what you could owe if you get it wrong.

## Why it matters

For **employees**, you withhold income tax and the employee’s share of Social Security and Medicare, pay the employer’s matching share and federal unemployment tax, and file payroll returns such as Form 941.

For **independent contractors**, you generally don’t withhold or pay those taxes. Instead, you report what you paid on Form 1099-NEC, and the contractor pays their own self-employment tax.

If you treat an employee as a contractor, you can be held responsible for the employment taxes that should have been paid, plus penalties and interest.

## How the IRS decides

The label in a contract isn’t what counts. The IRS looks at the whole relationship, grouped into three areas:

1. **Behavioral control.** Do you control or have the right to control what the worker does and how they do it? Detailed instructions and training point toward an employee.
2. **Financial control.** Who controls the business side of the work? Contractors usually invest in their own tools, can make or lose money, offer their services to others, and are paid by the job rather than by the hour.
3. **Relationship.** Are there benefits like insurance or vacation pay? Is the work ongoing and a key part of your regular business? Those point toward an employee.

No single factor decides it. If you’re unsure, Form SS-8 asks the IRS for a determination.

## Paying contractors correctly

- Get a completed **Form W-9** from each contractor before you pay them.
- For payments made after 2025, file **Form 1099-NEC** for each contractor you pay **$2,000 or more** during the year. Send it to the contractor and the IRS by **January 31**.
- Keep records of what you paid and the work performed.

## A quick self-check

If a worker has set hours you choose, works only for you, uses your equipment, and does work central to your business, take a careful look before treating them as a contractor.

> Classification questions are common and fixable. If you’re unsure about a worker, it’s much easier to sort out before the year ends.
