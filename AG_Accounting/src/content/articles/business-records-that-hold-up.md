If you run a business, the IRS expects you to keep records that show your income and support your expenses. You don’t need anything fancy. What matters is that the records are complete, organized, and kept long enough.

## Separate business and personal money

Open a separate bank account, and ideally a separate card, for the business. When every business dollar runs through one account, your bank statements become a big part of your records, and you avoid sorting personal from business purchases later.

## What to keep

The IRS lists the kinds of records that support your return in [Publication 583](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-publication-583). For most small businesses that means:

- **Income:** invoices, deposit records, 1099 forms you receive, and records of cash sales
- **Expenses:** receipts, canceled checks or bank records, and invoices from vendors
- **Assets:** what you paid for equipment, vehicles, or property, when you bought it, and when you sold it
- **Payroll:** wages paid, taxes withheld, and copies of payroll tax returns if you have employees

A good receipt shows the amount, date, who you paid, and what it was for. If that last part isn’t obvious, write a short note on it.

## Vehicle use needs a log

If you deduct business use of a car, keep a mileage log with the date, destination, business purpose, and miles for each trip. For 2026, the business standard mileage rate was 72.5 cents a mile through June 30 and 76 cents from July 1. Commuting from home to your regular workplace doesn’t count. Our [mileage deduction calculator](/calculators/mileage-deduction) estimates the deduction.

## Meals and travel

Business meals are generally only 50% deductible, and you should note who attended and the business reason. Travel costs need receipts plus a record of the business purpose. See [Publication 463](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-publication-463) for the details.

## How long to keep them

In most cases, keep records for **three years** after you file. Keep employment tax records for at least **four years**, and records about assets until the time to amend has passed for the year you sell them. Our [record retention guide](/tax-center/record-retention) has the full list.

## A simple monthly routine

1. Save every receipt the day you get it, on paper or as a photo.
2. Once a month, match your bank and card statements to your receipts and invoices.
3. Note anything unusual while you still remember it.
4. Back up digital records somewhere other than your main computer.

> Ten minutes a week now can save hours in April, and a lot of stress if the IRS ever asks questions.
