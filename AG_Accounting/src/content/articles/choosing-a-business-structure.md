Choosing a business structure is partly a legal decision and partly a tax decision. Here’s how the most common choices for small businesses compare.

## Sole proprietorship

If you start doing business on your own and do nothing else, you’re a sole proprietor.

- **Taxes:** business income and expenses go on Schedule C with your personal return. You pay income tax and self-employment tax on the profit.
- **Paperwork:** the simplest option.
- **Liability:** there’s no legal separation between you and the business.

## Limited liability company (LLC)

An LLC is created under state law and can separate business liabilities from your personal assets.

- **Taxes:** by default, a single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship, and a multi-member LLC is taxed like a partnership. An LLC can also elect to be taxed as a corporation or an S corporation.
- **Paperwork:** state formation filings plus ongoing upkeep.

For tax purposes, forming an LLC alone usually doesn’t change your federal tax bill. The liability protection is the main reason many owners choose it.

## Partnership

Two or more owners doing business together without incorporating usually form a partnership. The partnership files Form 1065, and each partner reports their share of income on their own return, generally paying self-employment tax on it.

## S corporation

An S corporation is a corporation (or an LLC) that elects to pass its income through to its owners.

- **Taxes:** the business files Form 1120-S. Owners who work in the business must be paid a **reasonable salary** through payroll. Profit beyond that salary passes through to the owners and isn’t subject to self-employment tax.
- **Paperwork:** payroll, a separate business return, and more formal recordkeeping.

The potential savings on self-employment tax are why many profitable small businesses consider an S corporation. But the added costs of payroll and return preparation, and the reasonable salary requirement, mean it doesn’t pay off for every business.

## C corporation

A C corporation pays its own income tax, and owners pay tax again on dividends. It’s more common for businesses planning to raise outside investment.

## Questions to ask before you decide

- How much profit do you expect, now and in a few years?
- How much personal liability protection do you need?
- Are you ready to run payroll and keep more formal records?
- Will you have partners or investors?

> Your structure can usually be changed later, but some changes have tax consequences. It’s worth talking it through before you file anything with the state.
