Most people pay someone to prepare their taxes, and most preparers are honest and skilled. But the law makes **you** responsible for what’s on your return, so it’s worth choosing carefully.

## Check their credentials

- Every paid preparer must have a **Preparer Tax Identification Number (PTIN)** and include it on returns they prepare.
- **Enrolled agents, CPAs and attorneys** can represent clients on any tax matter before the IRS, including audits, collections and appeals.
- Other preparers have more limited rights to represent you.

The IRS’s [choosing a tax professional page](https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/choosing-a-tax-professional) explains the differences and links to a directory of preparers with credentials.

## Ask about fees up front

Avoid preparers who base their fee on a **percentage of your refund**, or who promise a bigger refund than other preparers.

## Red flags

Be wary of a preparer who:

- **Won’t sign the return** or include their PTIN. These are sometimes called “ghost preparers.”
- Wants your refund deposited into **their** bank account instead of yours
- Asks you to **sign a blank return** or one you haven’t reviewed
- Files without your W-2 or other income documents, using a pay stub instead
- Invents income or credits to increase your refund

## Signs of a good preparer

- They ask for **all** your records and ask questions about your situation.
- They’re available **after** filing season if the IRS sends a letter.
- They explain your return and give you a copy.
- They protect your personal information.

## Before you sign

Review the return. Check that your name, Social Security number, income, and bank information are correct. Make sure the refund is going to your account. Keep a copy.
