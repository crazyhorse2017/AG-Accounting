Whether your Social Security benefits are taxed depends on how much **other income** you have. Many retirees owe nothing on their benefits; others pay federal tax on up to **85%** of them.

## The quick test

Add up:

1. Half of your Social Security benefits for the year, plus
2. All of your other income, including pensions, wages, IRA withdrawals, interest and dividends, plus
3. Any tax-exempt interest.

This total is often called your **provisional income**. Then compare it with these amounts:

- **Single, head of household:** under $25,000, none of your benefits are taxable. Between $25,000 and $34,000, up to 50% may be taxable. Over $34,000, up to 85% may be taxable.
- **Married filing jointly:** under $32,000, none are taxable. Between $32,000 and $44,000, up to 50%. Over $44,000, up to 85%.
- **Married filing separately** and living with your spouse at any time during the year: up to 85% is generally taxable.

These thresholds are set by law and aren’t adjusted for inflation, which is why more retirees owe tax on benefits over time.

## What 85% means

“Up to 85% taxable” doesn’t mean an 85% tax. It means up to 85% of your benefits are added to your taxable income and taxed at your normal rate.

## Ohio and your city

Ohio doesn’t tax Social Security benefits, and Ohio cities generally don’t either. Your federal return is where this matters.

## Planning ideas

- **Withholding:** you can have federal tax withheld from benefits by filing Form W-4V with the Social Security Administration.
- **Timing withdrawals:** large IRA or 401(k) withdrawals in one year can push more of your benefits into taxable income. Spreading them out can help.
- **New senior deduction:** from 2025 through 2028, people 65 and older may deduct an extra $6,000 each, subject to income limits. It doesn’t change how benefits are taxed, but it can lower the tax you owe.
