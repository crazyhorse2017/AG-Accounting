Filing status is one of the first choices on your tax return, and it matters more than many people realize. It decides which tax brackets apply to you, the size of your standard deduction, and whether you can claim certain credits.

## Your status on December 31 counts

Your marital status on the **last day of the year** generally decides your options for the whole year. If you married on December 31, you’re treated as married for that entire year.

## The five filing statuses

**Single.** For people who are unmarried, divorced, or legally separated at year-end and don’t qualify for another status.

**Married filing jointly.** Spouses combine their income and deductions on one return. This usually produces the lowest tax for married couples. Both spouses are responsible for the tax on a joint return, even if only one had income.

**Married filing separately.** Each spouse files a separate return. This often means higher tax and the loss of some credits, but it can make sense when one spouse has large medical expenses, when spouses want separate responsibility, or in other specific situations. If one spouse itemizes, the other must itemize too.

**Head of household.** For unmarried people who pay more than half the cost of keeping up a home for a qualifying person, such as a child. It offers a bigger standard deduction and wider brackets than single status: for 2026, the standard deduction is **$24,150** instead of **$16,100**.

**Qualifying surviving spouse.** For up to two years after the year a spouse dies, someone who has a dependent child and meets other tests can use joint-return rates and the joint standard deduction.

## When you have a choice

You may qualify for more than one status. You can generally choose the one that gives you the lowest tax. Common examples:

- A married couple comparing **jointly vs. separately**.
- A single parent who may qualify for **head of household**, which is often overlooked.
- Someone recently widowed who may qualify as a **surviving spouse**.

## Head of household: the details matter

Head of household has specific tests: you must be considered unmarried, you must have paid more than half the cost of the home for the year, and a qualifying person must have lived with you for more than half the year (with some exceptions, such as a dependent parent who lives elsewhere). Claiming it incorrectly is a common error, so it’s worth checking.

> Not sure which status fits? Bring it up at your appointment. A quick look at your household often settles it.
