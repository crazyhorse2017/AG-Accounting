Rental income is taxable, but landlords can deduct many of the costs of owning and running a rental. Most individual landlords report it all on **Schedule E**.

## What counts as rental income

Rent payments are income, and so are advance rent, fees a tenant pays for breaking a lease, and expenses a tenant pays on your behalf. A security deposit you plan to return isn’t income, but it becomes income if you keep it.

## Common deductions

- Mortgage interest on the rental
- Property taxes
- Insurance
- Repairs and maintenance
- Utilities you pay
- Property management fees
- Advertising, legal and accounting fees
- Travel to the property for rental business

## Repairs vs. improvements

This is where many landlords get tripped up.

- A **repair** keeps the property in good working order, like fixing a leak or repainting. Repairs are generally deductible in the year you pay for them.
- An **improvement** makes the property better, restores it, or adapts it to a new use, like a new roof or a room addition. Improvements are depreciated over time instead of deducted all at once.

## Depreciation

You can’t deduct the cost of the building all at once, but you can **depreciate** it. Residential rental buildings are generally depreciated over **27.5 years**. Land is never depreciated, so you need to separate the cost of the building from the land.

Depreciation lowers your taxable income each year, but it also lowers your basis, which can increase your taxable gain when you sell.

## Losses and the passive activity rules

Rental activities are generally **passive**, which limits deducting losses against other income. Many landlords who **actively participate**, for example by approving tenants and setting rent, can deduct up to **$25,000** of rental losses a year. That allowance phases out as modified adjusted gross income rises from $100,000 to $150,000. Losses you can’t use carry forward.

## Renting part of the year

If you rent out a home you also use personally, the rules change. Renting it for **fewer than 15 days** in a year generally means the rent isn’t taxable, and rental expenses aren’t deductible. Longer rentals mixed with personal use require splitting expenses.

## Keep good records

Save receipts and invoices, and keep a record of the purchase price, closing costs, and every improvement. You’ll need them for depreciation and when you sell.
