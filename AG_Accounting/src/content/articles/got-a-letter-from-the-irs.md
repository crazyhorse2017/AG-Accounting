An envelope from the IRS can make anyone’s heart sink. But most IRS letters are routine: a question about a number, a change to your refund, or a reminder about a balance. Here’s how to handle one calmly.

## Step 1: Make sure it’s real

The IRS usually contacts people first by **mail**. Real IRS letters have a **notice or letter number** (such as CP2000) in the top or bottom right corner. To be safe, check that the letter matches what you see in your **IRS Online Account**, or call the IRS using a phone number from IRS.gov, not one printed in a suspicious letter.

## Step 2: Read it carefully

Look for:

- **What the IRS thinks is wrong**, and the tax year it’s about
- **What they want you to do**
- **The deadline** to respond

Common letters include notices that the IRS changed your refund, that income on a form doesn’t match your return, or that you owe a balance.

## Step 3: Compare it with your return

Pull out the return for that year. Sometimes the IRS is right, for example when a 1099 was left off. Sometimes it’s missing information you can provide.

## Step 4: Respond by the deadline

- **If you agree**, follow the instructions, which may include paying.
- **If you disagree**, respond in writing with an explanation and copies (never originals) of documents that support your position.
- **If you can’t pay in full**, you can often set up a payment plan, many of them online.

Missing the deadline can limit your options, including your right to appeal.

## Step 5: Keep copies

Keep a copy of the letter, your response, and proof of when you sent it.

## You have rights

Every taxpayer has the right to be informed, to challenge the IRS’s position, and to appeal most decisions. If a problem isn’t being resolved through normal channels, the **Taxpayer Advocate Service** offers free help.

> Bring us any letter you receive, even if it looks minor. Many are quick to resolve, and a response written by a professional often settles things faster.
