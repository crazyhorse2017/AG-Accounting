Self-employed people who work from home may be able to deduct part of the cost of their home. The rules are specific, so it’s worth checking them before you claim it.

## Who qualifies

To claim the deduction, you generally need to use part of your home:

- **Regularly and exclusively** for your business. A spare room that’s only an office qualifies; a kitchen table that’s also used for dinner doesn’t.
- As your **principal place of business**, or a place where you regularly meet clients, or in a separate structure, like a detached garage workshop, used for the business.

There are limited exceptions to the exclusive-use rule, such as storing inventory or running a daycare.

**Employees** who work from home generally can’t take this deduction on their federal return, even if their employer requires it.

## Two ways to figure it

### The simplified method

Multiply the square footage of your office by **$5**, up to **300 square feet**. The maximum deduction is $1,500. There’s no depreciation to track and no need to add up home expenses. Our [home office calculator](/calculators/home-office-simplified) does it in seconds.

### The regular method

Figure the percentage of your home used for business, then deduct that share of actual expenses, such as:

- Mortgage interest or rent
- Property taxes and insurance
- Utilities
- Repairs and maintenance
- Depreciation on the business part of the home

You use **Form 8829** for the regular method. It often gives a bigger deduction for larger offices or higher-cost homes, but it takes more records.

## Limits to know

- The deduction generally can’t be more than the income from the business. Unused amounts under the regular method can carry forward.
- Under the regular method, depreciation you claimed may be taxed when you sell the home.
- You can choose either method each year.

## Keep records

Measure the space, take a photo, and keep records showing how you use it. Under the regular method, keep receipts for home expenses.
