Self-employed people and business owners can deduct the business use of a vehicle. You can figure the deduction in one of two ways.

## Method 1: Standard mileage rate

Multiply your business miles by the IRS standard mileage rate. For 2026, the rate was **72.5 cents** a mile from January through June and **76 cents** a mile from July 1 through December 31.

- Simple: you mainly need a mileage log.
- You can still deduct **business parking and tolls** separately.
- The rate covers gas, insurance, repairs, and depreciation, so you can’t deduct those too.

Our [mileage deduction calculator](/calculators/mileage-deduction) applies both 2026 rates.

## Method 2: Actual expenses

Add up what it actually costs to run the vehicle, then deduct the business-use percentage. Costs include:

- Gas and oil
- Repairs, tires and maintenance
- Insurance and registration
- Lease payments, or depreciation if you own the vehicle
- Interest on a car loan (for self-employed people)

This can give a bigger deduction for expensive vehicles or high operating costs, but it takes more records.

## Choosing a method

There are rules about switching. If you own the car and want to use the standard mileage rate, you generally have to use it in the **first year** the car is used for business. If you lease, and you choose the standard mileage rate, you must use it for the **entire lease**.

## What counts as business miles

- Driving between business locations, to clients, or to pick up supplies counts.
- **Commuting** from home to your regular workplace doesn’t count.
- If your home is your principal place of business, trips from home to other business stops generally count.

## The mileage log

The IRS expects a record made at or near the time of each trip, showing:

- Date
- Destination
- Business purpose
- Miles driven

Also record your odometer reading at the start and end of the year, so you can show total miles. A notebook in the glove box or a phone app both work.
