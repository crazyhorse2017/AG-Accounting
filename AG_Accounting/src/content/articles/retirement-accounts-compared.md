Retirement accounts are one of the simplest ways to lower your taxes while saving for the future. The question is usually which ones to use, and whether to choose **traditional** or **Roth**.

## Traditional vs. Roth: when you pay tax

- **Traditional** contributions are made before tax (or are deductible), so they lower your taxable income now. Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.
- **Roth** contributions are made after tax, so there’s no deduction now. Qualified withdrawals in retirement, including the growth, are tax-free.

A simple rule of thumb: if you expect your tax rate to be **lower** in retirement, traditional tends to come out ahead. If you expect it to be **higher**, Roth tends to win. Many people use both to spread their risk. Our [Roth vs. traditional calculator](/calculators/roth-vs-traditional) lets you compare.

## Workplace plans: 401(k), 403(b) and 457

For 2026 you can contribute up to **$24,500**, plus an **$8,000** catch-up if you’re 50 or older, or **$11,250** if you’re 60 through 63. Contributions come straight out of your paycheck.

The biggest advantage is often the **employer match**. If your employer matches contributions, contributing at least enough to get the full match is usually the first step. Our [401(k) calculator](/calculators/401k-growth) shows what the match adds up to.

## IRAs

Anyone with earned income (or a spouse with earned income) can contribute to an IRA. For 2026 the limit is **$7,500**, or **$8,600** if you’re 50 or older. That limit is shared between traditional and Roth IRAs.

- A **traditional IRA** deduction may be limited if you or your spouse are covered by a workplace plan and your income is above certain levels.
- **Roth IRA** contributions are only allowed below certain income levels.

You can make IRA contributions for 2026 up until the filing deadline in April 2027.

## Rules to know about withdrawals

- Withdrawals before age **59½** generally face a **10% additional tax** on top of regular income tax, unless an exception applies.
- Traditional accounts require **minimum distributions** starting at age 73 (75 for people born in 1960 or later).
- Roth IRAs don’t require minimum distributions while the original owner is alive.

## A common order of priorities

1. Contribute enough to your workplace plan to get the full employer match.
2. Consider an IRA, traditional or Roth depending on your situation.
3. Go back to your workplace plan for any additional savings.

> The right mix depends on your income, your tax rate now and later, and your other goals. We’re glad to help you run the numbers.
