If you sell your main home for more than you paid, you may be able to exclude much or all of the gain from tax.

## The exclusion

You can exclude up to **$250,000** of gain, or up to **$500,000** for most married couples filing jointly, if you meet these tests:

- **Ownership:** you owned the home for at least **two years** during the five years before the sale.
- **Use:** you lived in it as your main home for at least **two years** during those five years. The two years don’t need to be continuous.
- **Timing:** you generally haven’t used the exclusion for another home sale in the two years before this one.

For married couples to get the full $500,000, either spouse can meet the ownership test, but **both** must meet the use test.

## Partial exclusion

If you don’t meet the two-year tests because you moved for a **job change, health reasons, or certain unforeseen events**, you may still qualify for a reduced exclusion.

## Figuring your gain

Your gain is the sale price, minus selling costs, minus your **basis**. Basis is generally what you paid for the home, plus certain purchase costs, plus the cost of **improvements**, such as a new roof, an addition, or a remodeled kitchen.

This is why it pays to keep records of improvements for as long as you own the home. Every documented improvement lowers your gain. Our [home sale calculator](/calculators/home-sale-gain-exclusion) estimates the taxable part.

## What if the gain is bigger than the exclusion?

Gain above the exclusion is generally taxed as a **capital gain**, at long-term rates if you owned the home more than a year.

## Losses

A loss on selling your personal home **isn’t deductible**.

## Reporting the sale

If all of the gain is excluded and you didn’t receive a **Form 1099-S**, you may not need to report the sale. If you received a 1099-S, or have taxable gain, you report the sale on your return.

## Special situations

- If you used part of the home for a **business or rental**, depreciation you took after May 6, 1997 may be taxable when you sell.
- Rules differ for homes that were rentals before becoming your main home, and for military and some government employees.
