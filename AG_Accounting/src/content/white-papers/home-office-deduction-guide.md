## Overview

If you run a business from your home, the IRS lets you deduct a share of the costs of that home, but only if the space meets specific tests. This guide explains the tests, the two ways to figure the deduction, and the effects when you later sell your home.

## Who can claim it

The home office deduction is available to self-employed people, including sole proprietors, independent contractors, and partners who pay home office costs that aren’t reimbursed. **Employees** generally can’t deduct home office costs on their federal return, even when they work from home full time.

## The use tests

To qualify, part of your home must be used:

**Regularly.** Occasional or incidental business use doesn’t count.

**Exclusively.** The space is used only for business. A guest room that doubles as an office, or a family computer desk, generally fails this test. The space doesn’t need to be a separate room, but it must be an identifiable area used only for business.

**And as one of the following:**

- Your **principal place of business**. A home office can qualify even if you work elsewhere, as long as you use it for administrative or management activities and have no other fixed location where you do substantial administrative work.
- A place where you **meet clients or customers** in the normal course of business.
- A **separate structure**, not attached to your home, used in your business.

Two exceptions relax the exclusive-use rule: space used to store **inventory or product samples** when your home is the only fixed location of your business, and space used for a **licensed daycare**.

## Method 1: The simplified method

Multiply the square footage of qualifying space by **$5**, up to **300 square feet**, for a maximum deduction of **$1,500**.

Advantages:

- No need to track actual home expenses for the office
- No depreciation, so nothing is recaptured when you sell
- Mortgage interest and property taxes stay fully deductible as itemized deductions if you itemize

Limits: the deduction can’t create a loss, and unused amounts don’t carry forward.

## Method 2: The regular method

Figure the business percentage of your home, usually the office’s square footage divided by the home’s total square footage. Then apply that percentage to:

- **Indirect expenses** for the whole home: mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, utilities, general repairs, and rent if you rent
- **Depreciation** on the business part of the home, if you own it

Add **direct expenses** that apply only to the office, such as painting or repairing that room, in full.

You use **Form 8829** for the regular method. The deduction is limited to the gross income from the business use of the home, minus other business expenses. Amounts you can’t deduct because of that limit carry forward to future years.

## Choosing a method

You can choose either method each year. The regular method often gives a larger deduction for bigger offices or higher-cost homes. The simplified method saves time and avoids depreciation recapture. If you switch from the regular method, special rules apply to depreciation and carryovers.

## Selling your home

If you used the regular method and claimed depreciation, the depreciation you took after May 6, 1997 is generally taxable when you sell the home, even if the rest of your gain qualifies for the home sale exclusion. If the office was in the same dwelling as your home, you generally don’t need to split the sale between business and personal use. A separate structure may need to be treated as a separate sale.

## Records to keep

- A floor plan or measurements of the office and the whole home
- Photos showing the space and how it’s used
- Receipts and statements for home expenses, if you use the regular method
- Records of depreciation claimed

## Common mistakes

1. Claiming a space that isn’t used exclusively for business
2. Claiming the deduction as an employee
3. Deducting more than the business income allows
4. Forgetting depreciation recapture when selling
