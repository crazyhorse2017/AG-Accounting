## Why records matter

Good records do three jobs. They help you **run the business**, because you can see what’s profitable. They let you **prepare accurate tax returns**. And they **support your deductions** if the IRS ever asks questions. If you can’t show proof of an expense, the deduction can be disallowed.

The IRS doesn’t require any particular system. It requires records that clearly show your income and expenses.

## Set up the foundation

1. **Open a separate business bank account,** and ideally a separate card. Run every business dollar through it.
2. **Choose a simple bookkeeping method:** a spreadsheet, bookkeeping software, or a paper ledger, as long as you use it consistently.
3. **Pick one place for documents,** a folder system on paper or in the cloud, organized by year and by type.

## Records to keep

**Income**

- Invoices and sales records
- Bank deposit records
- Forms 1099-NEC and 1099-K you receive
- Records of cash and check payments

**Expenses**

- Receipts, invoices, and canceled checks or bank records
- Credit card statements
- Records showing the business purpose of each expense when it isn’t obvious

**Assets**

- Purchase records for equipment, vehicles, furniture, and property, including the date, cost, and how the item is used
- Records of depreciation and of any sale or disposal

**Employees**, if you have them

- Wages paid, tax withheld, and copies of W-2s and payroll returns
- Forms W-4 and I-9

## Special records the IRS expects

**Vehicle use.** Keep a mileage log recording the date, destination, business purpose, and miles for each business trip, along with your total miles for the year.

**Travel.** Keep receipts plus a record of the business purpose, dates, and places.

**Meals.** Note who you met with and the business reason. Business meals are generally only 50% deductible.

**Gifts.** Business gifts are deductible only up to $25 per person per year, so record who received them.

**Home office.** Keep measurements of the space and, if you use the regular method, records of home expenses.

## How long to keep records

- **Most records:** at least three years after you file the return.
- **Employment tax records:** at least four years after the tax is due or paid.
- **Asset records:** until the period to amend has passed for the year you sell or dispose of the asset.
- **If you didn’t file or a return was fraudulent:** there is no time limit.

Keep copies of your filed tax returns permanently; they’re useful for future returns and for figuring basis.

## A simple routine

**Every day or week**

- Photograph or file each receipt as you get it.
- Log mileage when you drive.

**Every month**

- Match bank and card statements to your records.
- Record any cash income.
- Note unusual items while you still remember them.

**Every quarter**

- Review profit, and set aside or pay estimated taxes.

**Every year**

- Close out the year, organize the folder, and bring it to your tax appointment.
- Back up digital records somewhere other than your main computer.

## Digital records

Electronic records are acceptable if they are accurate, complete, and readable. Scanned or photographed receipts are fine. Make sure backups are secure, because business records often include sensitive information.

## The payoff

A consistent system turns tax season from a scramble into a short review. It also helps you see where your money goes all year. AG Accounting can help you set up a system that fits the way you work.
