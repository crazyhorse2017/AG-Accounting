## What counts as gig work

Gig work is paid through an app or website: driving for rideshare, delivering food or packages, renting out a room or a car, freelancing, doing tasks, or selling goods and services online. Whether it’s your main job or a side hustle, the income is **taxable**, even if it’s small and even if you don’t receive a tax form.

## Employee or independent contractor?

Most gig workers are **independent contractors**. That means:

- No one withholds income tax or Social Security and Medicare tax from your pay.
- You report your income and expenses on **Schedule C**.
- You pay **self-employment tax** on your net earnings.
- You may need to make **quarterly estimated tax payments**.

## Forms you might receive

- **Form 1099-NEC:** from a business that paid you for services.
- **Form 1099-K:** from payment apps, online marketplaces or card processors that handled payments to you.
- **Form 1099-MISC:** for certain other payments, such as rents.

The amounts on these forms are often **gross** payments, before platform fees and other costs. You still report all your income, then deduct expenses on Schedule C.

Selling your own used personal items for less than you paid generally isn’t taxable, even if it’s reported on a 1099-K, but you should be able to show that.

## Deductible expenses

You pay tax on **profit**, so track every legitimate business cost:

**Vehicle expenses.** Use either the standard mileage rate (72.5 cents a mile for January through June 2026 and 76 cents from July 1) or actual expenses for the business share of the vehicle. Keep a mileage log with dates, destinations, purposes and miles. Miles driven while waiting for or traveling to a ride or delivery request may count, but commuting doesn’t.

**Platform fees and commissions** taken out of your pay.

**Phone and data**, for the share used for work.

**Supplies and equipment**, such as insulated delivery bags, phone mounts, chargers, cleaning supplies, or tools.

**Home office**, if you have space used regularly and exclusively for the business.

**Other costs** like parking and tolls for business trips, licenses and insurance required for the work.

## Self-employment tax

Self-employment tax is **15.3%** of 92.35% of your net earnings, covering Social Security and Medicare. It applies once net earnings reach $400. Half of it is deductible. If you also have a W-2 job, your wages count toward the Social Security wage base first.

## Tips for gig workers

From 2025 through 2028, people in occupations that customarily receive tips may deduct up to $25,000 of qualified tips, subject to income limits. Rules for self-employed people limit the deduction to the net income of the business where the tips were earned.

## Paying as you go

Because nothing is withheld:

1. Set aside a fixed percentage of each payout, often 25% to 30% to start, in a separate account.
2. Make estimated payments on **April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15**.
3. If you also have a job, you can increase W-4 withholding instead.

## Ohio and city taxes

Gig income is taxable on your Ohio return, and most Ohio cities tax net self-employment profit. Gig workers usually owe city tax where they live, and sometimes where they work, and no one withholds it for them. Many cities expect estimated payments.

## Records to keep

- Statements and annual summaries from each platform
- 1099 forms
- A mileage log
- Receipts for expenses
- Bank records for the account you use for gig income

## Common mistakes

- Reporting only the amounts on 1099 forms and missing other income
- Forgetting to deduct platform fees that were taken out before you were paid
- Skipping estimated payments
- Not keeping a mileage log
- Forgetting city income tax
