## Why year-end matters for businesses

For most small businesses, the tax year ends December 31. Decisions made in November and December, about spending, billing, retirement contributions and payroll, can change this year’s tax bill. This checklist covers the common moves; which make sense depends on your business and your expected income next year.

## 1. Get your books current

Reconcile bank and card accounts through the latest month, record all income and expenses, and review profit year to date. Planning is only as good as the numbers behind it.

## 2. Project this year and next

Compare this year’s expected profit with next year’s. If you expect to be in a higher bracket next year, it may make sense to accelerate income into this year. If this year is the high-income year, consider deferring income and accelerating deductions.

## 3. Timing income and expenses

Most small businesses use the **cash method**, which counts income when received and expenses when paid.

- **Deferring income:** sending some invoices in late December instead of mid-month may push payment into next year. Be careful: income you’ve already received or that’s available to you is taxable now.
- **Accelerating expenses:** paying bills, buying supplies you’ll use soon, or paying for services before year-end can increase this year’s deductions. Prepaying expenses that cover far into the future generally isn’t deductible all at once.

## 4. Equipment purchases

Buying and placing equipment, vehicles, computers or furniture in service before December 31 may let you deduct much or all of the cost this year, through **Section 179 expensing** or bonus depreciation, instead of over several years. For 2025, the Section 179 limit was **$2,500,000**. Buy what the business needs; a deduction never makes an unnecessary purchase free.

## 5. Retirement plans for owners

Retirement contributions can be one of the biggest deductions available:

- **Solo 401(k):** for owners without employees other than a spouse. Deadlines for setting up a new plan and making employee deferrals depend on how the business is organized, and recent law changes gave some sole proprietors more time, so check the timing before year-end.
- **SEP IRA:** can be set up and funded as late as the tax return due date, including extensions.
- **SIMPLE IRA:** has earlier setup deadlines for the following year.

## 6. Qualified business income deduction

Owners of pass-through businesses may deduct up to 20% of qualified business income. At higher incomes, the deduction can depend on W-2 wages paid and business property. Year-end decisions about wages, retirement contributions and purchases can affect it.

## 7. Payroll and year-end forms

- Confirm employee names, addresses and Social Security numbers for **Forms W-2**, due to employees and the government by **January 31**.
- Collect **Form W-9** from every contractor now. For payments made after 2025, file **Form 1099-NEC** for contractors paid **$2,000 or more** during the year, by **January 31**.
- If you’re an S corporation owner, make sure you’ve paid yourself a **reasonable salary** through payroll.
- Review fringe benefits that need to be included in wages, such as personal use of a company vehicle.

## 8. Estimated taxes

Review federal, Ohio and city estimated payments. The fourth-quarter federal estimate is due in mid-January. An increase now can avoid an underpayment penalty.

## 9. Inventory and receivables

- Count inventory at year-end if you carry it.
- Review old customer balances. Under the accrual method, truly uncollectible debts may be deductible. Under the cash method, unpaid invoices were never income, so there’s nothing to write off.

## 10. Organize records

- Gather receipts and statements
- Update the mileage log and record year-end odometer readings
- Save records of equipment purchases and the date each was placed in service

## Ohio and city taxes

Ohio taxes business income on the individual return at a flat 3% rate after the business income deduction, and most cities tax net business profit. Changes that lower federal income generally lower these taxes too.
