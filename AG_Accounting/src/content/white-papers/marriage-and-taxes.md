## Married for the whole year

Your marital status on **December 31** generally applies to the whole year. If you marry on New Year’s Eve, you’re considered married for that entire tax year.

## Filing status: jointly or separately

Married couples choose between two filing statuses.

**Married filing jointly**

- Combines both spouses’ income, deductions and credits on one return
- Usually produces the lowest total tax
- Gives the largest standard deduction: **$32,200** for 2026
- Makes both spouses legally responsible for the tax, interest and penalties on the return

**Married filing separately**

- Each spouse reports their own income and deductions
- Often results in higher tax, and some benefits aren’t available, including the education credits, the student loan interest deduction, and in most cases the Earned Income Tax Credit
- If one spouse itemizes, the other must too
- Can make sense when one spouse has high medical expenses, when spouses want to keep tax liability separate, or in some cases involving income-driven student loan repayment

It’s often worth comparing both ways.

## Update your withholding

Two incomes combined can push you into a higher bracket than either had alone. Both spouses should submit a new **Form W-4**. Step 2 of the W-4 is designed for households with two jobs. The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator can help you get it right.

## Name changes

If you change your name, update it with the **Social Security Administration** before you file. The name and Social Security number on your return must match SSA records, or your return can be delayed.

## Address changes

If you move, notify the IRS with **Form 8822** and update your address with the post office, your employer, and Ohio.

## Health insurance through the Marketplace

If either spouse gets health insurance through the Marketplace with advance premium tax credits, report the marriage to the Marketplace. Your combined income may change the credit you qualify for.

## Retirement savings

- A spouse with little or no income can still contribute to an IRA based on the working spouse’s income, through a **spousal IRA**.
- Whether a traditional IRA contribution is deductible may change if your spouse is covered by a workplace retirement plan.
- Update the **beneficiaries** on retirement accounts and life insurance.

## Selling a home

A married couple filing jointly may exclude up to **$500,000** of gain on selling their main home, compared with $250,000 for a single person, if both spouses meet the residence test and either meets the ownership test.

## Credits and phase-outs

Many credits and deductions phase out at income levels that are higher for joint filers but not always double the single amount. Combining incomes can reduce or eliminate some benefits you each received while single, such as the Earned Income Tax Credit.

## Ohio and your city

Ohio generally requires spouses to use the same filing status on the state return as on the federal return. Each spouse’s city income tax is based on where that spouse lives and works.

## A checklist for newlyweds

1. Update names with Social Security
2. File new W-4s with both employers
3. Report the change to the Marketplace, if applicable
4. Update addresses with the IRS, Ohio and the post office
5. Review retirement and insurance beneficiaries
6. Compare filing jointly and separately at tax time
