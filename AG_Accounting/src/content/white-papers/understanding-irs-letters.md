## Don’t panic, but don’t ignore it

The IRS sends millions of letters every year. Most deal with a specific, fixable issue: a balance due, a change to your return, a question about a number, or a request to verify your identity. Responding on time almost always gives you more options and a better result.

## Is it really from the IRS?

Real IRS letters:

- Arrive by **mail**, usually as the first contact about an issue
- Show a **notice or letter number** (such as CP14 or Letter 5071C) in the top or bottom right corner
- Never demand payment by gift card, wire transfer or cryptocurrency

To check, compare the letter with your **IRS Online Account**, or call the IRS at a number listed on IRS.gov rather than one printed in a suspicious message.

## Reading the letter

Every notice tells you:

1. **The tax year** involved
2. **What the IRS changed or wants**
3. **What you need to do**
4. **The deadline** to respond
5. **Your rights**, including how to disagree

## Common notices

**CP14: balance due.** You owe tax on a return, often because a payment was short or late. Pay in full, or set up a payment plan. If you think the balance is wrong, contact the IRS by the deadline.

**CP11 and CP12: math error corrections.** The IRS corrected a mistake on your return, which changed your balance or refund. If you disagree, you generally have **60 days** to ask the IRS to reverse the change.

**CP2000: proposed changes.** Income or payments reported to the IRS by employers, banks or brokers don’t match your return. This is a **proposal**, not a bill. You can agree, partly agree, or disagree with documentation. Respond by the date on the notice, usually within 30 days.

**Letter 5071C and similar: identity verification.** The IRS received a return with your name and number and wants to confirm it was you. Follow the instructions to verify online or by phone. If you didn’t file, say so; it may be identity theft.

**CP501, CP503, CP504: balance reminders.** These follow up on an unpaid balance, each more urgent than the last. A CP504 warns that the IRS may levy certain property, such as a state tax refund.

**CP3219A: notice of deficiency.** Sometimes called a 90-day letter. It proposes additional tax and gives you **90 days** to petition the U.S. Tax Court if you disagree. This deadline is set by law and can’t be extended.

**Final notice of intent to levy (such as Letter 1058 or LT11).** Before the IRS can levy wages or bank accounts, it must send this notice. You generally have **30 days** to request a Collection Due Process hearing.

## How to respond

- **If you agree:** follow the instructions and pay, or set up a payment plan.
- **If you disagree:** write a clear explanation and include **copies** (never originals) of supporting documents. Include the notice or a copy of it, and write your name, Social Security number and tax year on each page.
- **Keep records:** a copy of the notice, your response, and proof of mailing or upload.
- **Use the right address:** send responses to the address on the notice, or use the IRS’s document upload option when the notice offers it.

## Your rights

Every taxpayer has the right to be informed, to challenge the IRS’s position and be heard, and to appeal most decisions in an independent forum. The IRS Independent Office of Appeals handles many disputes without going to court. If a problem isn’t being resolved through normal channels, the **Taxpayer Advocate Service** offers free help.

## Ohio and city letters

Ohio and city tax agencies send their own notices with their own deadlines. Treat them with the same care.
