## Why plan before December 31

Once the year ends, most of your tax results are locked in. A review in the fall, while there’s still time to act, can lower your tax, avoid penalties, and prevent surprises. Not every idea fits every household; use this as a checklist to discuss.

## 1. Project your income and tax

Start with a rough estimate of this year’s income, deductions and tax. Note anything unusual: a job change, a bonus, a business that grew, a home sale, a large investment gain, or a new child. Comparing with last year shows whether you’re on track.

## 2. Check withholding and estimated payments

If you’re likely to owe, act before year-end:

- Increase withholding on a new **Form W-4** for your remaining paychecks. Withholding is treated as paid evenly through the year, which can help avoid an underpayment penalty.
- Make or increase the **fourth-quarter estimated payment**, due in mid-January.

The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator can help.

## 3. Retirement contributions

- **Workplace plans** (401(k), 403(b), 457): contributions must come out of a paycheck by December 31. For 2026, up to **$24,500**, plus **$8,000** at 50 or older, or **$11,250** at ages 60 to 63.
- **IRAs:** you have until the April filing deadline to contribute for 2026: up to **$7,500**, or **$8,600** at 50 or older.
- **Roth or traditional?** In a lower-income year, Roth contributions or a Roth conversion may make sense. In a high-income year, traditional contributions may save more now.

## 4. Required minimum distributions

If you’re required to take an RMD this year, take it by **December 31** (or April 1 for your very first one). Consider a **qualified charitable distribution** from an IRA if you’re 70½ or older and give to charity.

## 5. Investments

- **Harvest losses:** selling investments at a loss can offset gains, plus up to $3,000 of other income ($1,500 if married filing separately). Watch the wash sale rule if you buy back the same or substantially identical investment within 30 days.
- **Hold for long-term rates:** gains on investments held more than a year are taxed at lower rates. A few more weeks can make a difference.
- **Use the 0% rate:** in a low-income year, some long-term gains may be taxed at 0%.

## 6. Charitable giving

- Starting with 2026, people who don’t itemize may deduct up to **$1,000** of cash gifts to qualified charities (**$2,000** joint).
- If you itemize, **bunching** two years of gifts into one year can push you over the standard deduction.
- Giving appreciated stock you’ve held more than a year can avoid capital gains tax.
- Get written acknowledgments for gifts of $250 or more.

## 7. Health accounts

- Use remaining **flexible spending account** money before your plan’s deadline.
- Maximize **HSA** contributions if you’re eligible. You generally have until the filing deadline.

## 8. Education

Paying spring tuition by December 31 may let you count it for this year’s education credit. Review 529 withdrawals so they match qualified expenses in the same year.

## 9. Life changes

Marriage, divorce, a new child, a move, or retirement can change your filing status, credits and withholding. Update your W-4 and estimated payments.

## 10. Gather records early

Start a folder for next year’s tax documents: W-2s, 1099s, receipts and statements. Use our appointment checklist so nothing is missing.

## A note on Ohio and city taxes

Ohio and city taxes generally follow your federal income, but have their own estimated payment rules. If you had a big increase in income, check your state and city payments too.
