## Reporting rental income

Most individual landlords report rental income and expenses on **Schedule E**. Rental income includes:

- Regular rent payments
- **Advance rent**, in the year you receive it, no matter what period it covers
- Fees a tenant pays to **cancel a lease**
- Expenses a tenant pays for you, such as a repair bill
- The fair value of services a tenant provides instead of rent

A **security deposit** isn’t income when you receive it if you plan to return it. If you keep part of it because the tenant didn’t live up to the lease, that part becomes income.

## Deductible expenses

You can deduct ordinary and necessary expenses of managing, conserving and maintaining a rental, including:

- Mortgage interest
- Property taxes
- Insurance
- Repairs and maintenance
- Utilities you pay
- Advertising
- Management fees and commissions
- Legal and accounting fees
- Travel to the property for rental business
- Depreciation

## Repairs vs. improvements

A **repair** keeps the property in its ordinary operating condition. Fixing a leak, patching a wall, replacing a broken window pane, and repainting are typical repairs. Repairs are deducted in the year paid.

An **improvement** makes the property better, restores it after major wear, or adapts it to a new use. A new roof, a room addition, a new heating system, or a full kitchen remodel are improvements. They’re added to your basis and **depreciated** over time.

Certain small purchases can be deducted right away under elections such as the de minimis safe harbor, if you follow the rules.

## Depreciation

You recover the cost of a rental building, but not the land, through depreciation.

- Residential rental buildings are generally depreciated over **27.5 years**.
- Depreciation starts when the property is **placed in service**, meaning ready and available to rent.
- You must separate the cost of the building from the land, often using the county’s assessed values.
- Improvements, appliances and furniture have their own recovery periods.

Depreciation lowers your taxable income each year. When you sell, depreciation you were allowed to take reduces your basis and is generally taxed at up to 25%.

## The passive activity rules

Rental activities are generally **passive**, so losses usually can only offset passive income. Two important exceptions:

- **Active participation:** if you own at least 10% of the property and make management decisions such as approving tenants and setting rent, you may deduct up to **$25,000** of rental losses against other income. This allowance phases out as modified adjusted gross income rises from **$100,000 to $150,000**.
- **Real estate professionals** who meet strict hour tests can treat rental activities as non-passive.

Disallowed losses carry forward and are generally freed up when you sell the property.

## Personal use of a rental

If you or your family use a property that you also rent out:

- Renting it for **fewer than 15 days** during the year generally means the rent isn’t taxable, and rental expenses aren’t deductible.
- If you use it personally for more than the greater of **14 days** or **10%** of the days it’s rented at fair rent, it’s treated as a residence, expenses must be divided between rental and personal use, and rental losses are limited.

## Selling a rental

Gain on selling a rental is figured using your adjusted basis, which has been reduced by depreciation. Part of the gain from depreciation is taxed at up to 25%; the rest is generally a long-term capital gain if you owned the property more than a year. A like-kind exchange may defer the gain if you buy another investment property and follow strict rules.

## Ohio and city taxes

Rental income flows through to your Ohio return. Many Ohio cities tax net rental profit, and you may need to file with the city where the property is located as well as where you live.

## Records to keep

- Closing statements from the purchase
- A record of every improvement and its cost
- Depreciation schedules
- Rent rolls, leases and deposit records
- Receipts for all expenses
- Records of personal use days
