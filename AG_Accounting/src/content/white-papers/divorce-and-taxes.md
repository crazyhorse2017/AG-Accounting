## Why taxes matter in a divorce

A divorce or separation changes your filing status, who can claim the children, how support payments are taxed, and what happens to property and retirement accounts. Planning for these issues before the agreement is final can prevent costly surprises. This guide covers the federal rules in general; your attorney handles the legal agreement.

## Filing status

Your status on **December 31** controls the whole year.

- **Still married on December 31:** you file as married filing jointly or married filing separately, unless you qualify to be treated as unmarried.
- **Divorced or legally separated under a decree by December 31:** you file as single or, if you qualify, head of household.
- **Considered unmarried:** a married person who lived apart from their spouse for the last six months of the year and paid more than half the cost of keeping up a home for a qualifying child may be able to file as **head of household**.

If you file jointly in the year you separate, both spouses remain responsible for the tax on that return. Relief may be available in some cases for an innocent spouse.

## Claiming the children

Generally, the **custodial parent**, the parent the child lived with for more nights during the year, claims the child.

The custodial parent can let the noncustodial parent claim the child for the **Child Tax Credit** and credit for other dependents by signing **Form 8332** (or a substantially similar statement), which the noncustodial parent attaches to their return.

Even when Form 8332 is used, some benefits stay with the custodial parent:

- Head of household filing status
- The Earned Income Tax Credit
- The child and dependent care credit
- The exclusion for dependent care benefits

Agreements should say clearly who claims each child, and in which years.

## Alimony

For divorce or separation agreements executed **after 2018**, alimony is **not deductible** by the payer and **not taxable** to the recipient. Agreements executed before 2019 generally follow the old rules (deductible and taxable) unless they were modified to adopt the new rules.

## Child support

Child support is **never deductible** by the payer and **never taxable** to the recipient.

## Dividing property

Transfers of property between spouses, or between former spouses when the transfer is related to the divorce, are generally **not taxable**. The person receiving the property takes over the original **basis**. That means two assets with the same current value can carry very different future tax bills. A $100,000 brokerage account with a $20,000 basis carries much more future tax than $100,000 in cash.

## The home

- If you sell the home during or after the divorce, each spouse may be able to exclude up to **$250,000** of gain on their own return if they meet the ownership and use tests.
- If one spouse keeps living in the home under the divorce or separation instrument, the other may still be treated as using it as a residence.
- The spouse who keeps the home takes over its basis, which matters when it’s eventually sold.

## Retirement accounts

- **Workplace plans** such as 401(k)s are usually divided under a **qualified domestic relations order (QDRO)**. Distributions to a former spouse under a QDRO are taxed to the former spouse, and the early distribution tax doesn’t apply to them.
- **IRAs** can be transferred to a former spouse under a divorce decree without tax, if done as a direct transfer.

Withdrawing retirement money outside these procedures can trigger tax and penalties.

## After the divorce

1. File a new **Form W-4** with your employer.
2. Update your name with the Social Security Administration if it changed.
3. Update your address with the IRS using Form 8822.
4. Review **estimated tax payments**, especially if you receive taxable income from property you received.
5. Update beneficiaries on retirement accounts and insurance.
6. Report changes to the Marketplace if you get premium tax credits.

## Ohio

Ohio generally requires the same filing status on your Ohio return as on your federal return. If you move, city income tax obligations may change.
