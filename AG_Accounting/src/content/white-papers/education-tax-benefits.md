## The big picture

The tax code offers several ways to reduce the cost of education. The most valuable are two **credits** for college costs, tax-advantaged **529 savings plans**, the exclusion for **scholarships**, and the deduction for **student loan interest**. Because the same expenses generally can’t be used for more than one benefit, coordinating them matters.

## American Opportunity Tax Credit

- Up to **$2,500 per eligible student** each year: 100% of the first $2,000 of qualified expenses and 25% of the next $2,000
- Up to **40% refundable** (as much as $1,000), even if you owe no tax
- Available for the **first four years** of postsecondary education
- The student must be pursuing a degree or other recognized credential and be enrolled at least half-time for at least one academic period beginning in the year
- The student must not have a felony drug conviction
- Qualified expenses include tuition, required fees, and required course materials such as books and supplies, even if bought elsewhere

## Lifetime Learning Credit

- Up to **$2,000 per return**: 20% of up to $10,000 of qualified expenses
- **Not refundable**
- No limit on the number of years
- Covers undergraduate, graduate and professional degree courses, and courses taken to get or improve job skills
- Only one course is needed; there’s no half-time requirement

## Income limits and other rules

Both credits phase out for modified adjusted gross income between **$80,000 and $90,000**, or **$160,000 and $180,000** for married couples filing jointly. People who are married filing separately can’t claim either. You can’t claim both credits for the same student in the same year, but you can claim the AOTC for one student and the Lifetime Learning Credit for another.

If a parent can claim the student as a dependent, generally only the parent can claim the credit for that student.

## Form 1098-T

The school sends Form 1098-T showing tuition and scholarships. It’s a starting point, but your credit is based on what you **actually paid** during the year, so keep your own records and receipts.

## 529 plans

A 529 plan is an education savings account sponsored by a state or school.

- Contributions aren’t deductible on the federal return, but earnings grow tax-deferred.
- Withdrawals are **tax-free** when used for qualified education expenses, which include college tuition, fees, books, supplies, equipment, and room and board for students enrolled at least half-time. Some K-12 and apprenticeship expenses also qualify.
- Earnings withdrawn for other purposes are taxable and generally face an extra 10% tax.
- Ohio offers a state income tax deduction for contributions to Ohio’s 529 plan.

## Scholarships and grants

Scholarships and fellowship grants are generally **tax-free** if used for tuition, required fees, books, supplies and equipment by a degree candidate. Amounts used for room and board, travel, or optional expenses are taxable, as are payments for teaching or research services.

## Student loan interest

You may be able to deduct up to **$2,500** a year of interest on qualified student loans, even if you don’t itemize. The deduction phases out at higher incomes and isn’t available if you’re married filing separately or can be claimed as someone else’s dependent.

## Coordinating benefits

You can’t use the same dollars twice. For example:

- Expenses paid with **tax-free scholarships** can’t also be used for a credit.
- Expenses used to figure an **education credit** can’t also be used to make a 529 withdrawal tax-free.

A common strategy is to pay at least $4,000 of qualified expenses with money that isn’t from a 529 plan or tax-free scholarship, to claim the full American Opportunity Tax Credit, and use 529 money for the rest. In some cases, treating part of a scholarship as taxable income to unlock a larger credit saves money overall.

## Records to keep

- Forms 1098-T and 1098-E
- Receipts for tuition, fees, books and supplies
- 529 plan statements and Form 1099-Q
- Scholarship award letters
