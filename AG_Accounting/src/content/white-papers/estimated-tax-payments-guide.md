## Why estimated taxes exist

The federal income tax is a pay-as-you-go tax. Employees pay through withholding. When income isn’t subject to withholding, such as self-employment income, rental income, investment gains or some retirement income, you’re expected to pay during the year through quarterly **estimated tax payments**.

## Who should pay

Generally, you need to make estimated payments if you expect to owe **$1,000 or more** when you file, after subtracting withholding and refundable credits, and your withholding won’t meet the safe harbor described below. Corporations follow different rules.

People who commonly make estimated payments include:

- Sole proprietors, freelancers and gig workers
- Partners and S corporation shareholders
- Landlords
- Investors with large dividends or capital gains
- Retirees whose pensions or IRA withdrawals have too little withheld

## When payments are due

For most people the four payments are due on:

1. **April 15:** for income earned January through March
2. **June 15:** for income earned April and May
3. **September 15:** for income earned June through August
4. **January 15 of the next year:** for income earned September through December

When a date falls on a weekend or legal holiday, the deadline moves to the next business day.

## How much to pay: the safe harbor

You can generally avoid an underpayment penalty if your withholding and timely estimated payments together equal at least the smaller of:

1. **90%** of the tax shown on this year’s return, or
2. **100%** of the tax shown on last year’s return, if it covered all 12 months. This becomes **110%** if last year’s adjusted gross income was more than $150,000 ($75,000 if married filing separately).

The prior-year method is popular because the number is known in advance. Divide the required amount by four and pay that each quarter.

Farmers and fishermen have special, more lenient rules.

## When income is uneven

If you earn most of your income late in the year, equal quarterly payments may be more than you need early on. The **annualized income installment method**, figured on Form 2210 Schedule AI, lets you match payments to when you actually earned the income and can reduce or eliminate a penalty.

## Withholding as an alternative

If you or your spouse also have a job, you can increase withholding on Form W-4 instead of, or in addition to, making estimated payments. Withholding is treated as paid evenly through the year, even if it’s taken late in the year, which can help fix a shortfall in the fourth quarter.

## How to pay federal estimates

- **IRS Direct Pay** from a bank account, at no cost
- Your **IRS Online Account**
- **EFTPS**, the Electronic Federal Tax Payment System
- Debit or credit card through approved processors (fees apply)
- Check or money order with a **Form 1040-ES** voucher

## Ohio and city estimates

Ohio expects estimated payments when you’ll owe more than a small amount of state tax, using Form IT 1040ES, generally on similar due dates. Most Ohio cities also expect estimated payments from residents with self-employment, rental or other income not covered by withholding. Each city or collection agency (such as RITA or CCA) has its own rules.

## The underpayment penalty

If you don’t pay enough, or pay late, the IRS figures a penalty that works like interest on each late installment, for the time it was late. The penalty may be waived if the underpayment was due to a casualty, disaster or other unusual circumstance, or in certain cases after retirement or disability.

## A simple system

1. Use last year’s total tax as a starting point.
2. Set aside a fixed percentage of every payment you receive in a separate savings account.
3. Put the four federal due dates, plus Ohio and city dates, on your calendar.
4. Revisit the amount mid-year if your income changes a lot.
