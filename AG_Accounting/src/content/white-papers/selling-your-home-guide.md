## The good news

Most people who sell their main home owe no federal income tax on the gain. The tax code lets you exclude up to **$250,000** of gain, or up to **$500,000** for most married couples filing jointly, if you meet a few tests.

## Step 1: Figure your gain

**Amount realized** = sale price minus selling expenses (such as real estate commissions, title fees and legal fees).

**Adjusted basis** = what you paid for the home, plus certain purchase costs, plus the cost of **improvements**, minus any depreciation claimed and certain other adjustments.

**Gain** = amount realized minus adjusted basis.

Improvements add to basis; ordinary repairs don’t. Improvements are things that add value, prolong the home’s life, or adapt it to new uses, such as an addition, a new roof, new windows, central air, a remodeled kitchen or bathroom, or a new driveway.

## Step 2: Check the eligibility tests

You can generally claim the full exclusion if:

- **Ownership:** you owned the home for at least **24 months** during the five years before the sale.
- **Residence:** you lived in the home as your main home for at least **24 months** during those five years. The months don’t need to be continuous.
- **Look-back:** you didn’t exclude the gain from another home sale during the two years before this sale.

For the **$500,000** exclusion on a joint return, either spouse can meet the ownership test, but **both** spouses must meet the residence test, and neither can have used the exclusion in the previous two years.

## Step 3: If you don’t fully qualify

You may still get a **partial exclusion** if the main reason you sold was:

- A change in workplace location
- Health reasons
- Certain unforeseeable events, such as a divorce, multiple births, or loss of a job

The partial exclusion is generally based on the share of the two-year period you met the tests.

## Special situations

**Surviving spouses.** A surviving spouse who hasn’t remarried may be able to claim the $500,000 exclusion if the home is sold within two years of the spouse’s death and the other tests were met.

**Divorce.** If your former spouse lived in the home under a divorce or separation instrument, you may be treated as using it as your residence during that time.

**Military and certain government employees.** People on qualified official extended duty can choose to suspend the five-year test period for up to 10 years.

**Business or rental use.** If you claimed depreciation for a home office or rental use after May 6, 1997, that depreciation is generally taxable when you sell, even if the rest of the gain is excluded. If part of the property was a separate business or rental structure, part of the sale may not qualify.

**Former rentals.** If the home was a rental before it became your main home, some gain may not qualify for the exclusion.

## Gain over the exclusion

Gain above the exclusion is a capital gain. If you owned the home more than one year, it’s taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates. It may also be subject to the 3.8% net investment income tax at higher incomes.

## Losses

A loss on the sale of your main home **isn’t deductible**.

## Reporting the sale

- If your entire gain is excluded and you didn’t receive **Form 1099-S**, you generally don’t need to report the sale.
- If you received a Form 1099-S, or part of your gain is taxable, report the sale on Form 8949 and Schedule D.

## Records to keep

Keep these until at least three years after you report the sale:

- Closing statements from your purchase and sale
- Receipts and invoices for every improvement
- Records of any depreciation for business or rental use

## Ohio

Ohio starts with federal adjusted gross income, so gain excluded on your federal return generally isn’t taxed by Ohio either. Ohio cities generally don’t tax gain on the sale of a home.
