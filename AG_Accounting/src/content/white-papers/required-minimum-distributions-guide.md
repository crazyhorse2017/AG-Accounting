## What an RMD is

Traditional retirement accounts let money grow tax-deferred for years, but not forever. Starting at a certain age, you must withdraw at least a minimum amount each year: your **required minimum distribution** (RMD). The withdrawal is generally taxed as ordinary income.

RMDs apply to traditional IRAs, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs, and most workplace plans such as 401(k) and 403(b) plans. **Roth IRAs** don’t require distributions while the original owner is alive, and starting in 2024, designated Roth accounts in workplace plans don’t either.

## When RMDs start

- **Age 73** for people born from 1951 through 1959
- **Age 75** for people born in 1960 or later

For workplace plans, if you’re still working and don’t own 5% or more of the business, the plan may let you wait until you retire.

## Deadlines

- Your **first** RMD is due by **April 1** of the year after you reach the required age.
- Every later RMD is due by **December 31**.

Delaying the first one until April means taking two RMDs in that year, which can push you into a higher bracket.

## How to figure the amount

For most people:

**RMD = account balance on December 31 of last year ÷ distribution period from the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table**

For example, at age 75 the distribution period is 24.6. A $500,000 balance means an RMD of about $20,325. Our RMD calculator does the math.

If your **spouse is your sole beneficiary and more than 10 years younger**, you use the Joint Life and Last Survivor table instead, which produces a smaller RMD.

## Multiple accounts

- **IRAs:** figure the RMD for each IRA separately, then you may take the total from any one or more of your IRAs.
- **403(b) accounts:** may be combined the same way.
- **401(k) and most other workplace plans:** the RMD must be taken from each plan separately.

## Missing an RMD

The additional tax for not taking a required distribution is **25%** of the amount not taken, reduced to **10%** if you correct it in a timely manner. You report it on **Form 5329**, and you can ask the IRS to waive it if the shortfall was due to reasonable error and you’re fixing it.

## Inherited accounts

Rules for inherited retirement accounts changed in 2020.

- A **surviving spouse** has several options, including treating the account as their own.
- Certain **eligible designated beneficiaries**, including a minor child of the owner, a disabled or chronically ill person, or someone not more than 10 years younger than the owner, may stretch distributions over their life expectancy.
- Most **other individual beneficiaries** must empty the account by the end of the **10th year** after the owner’s death, and may also need annual RMDs during those years if the owner had already started RMDs.

Inherited account rules are complex. Get advice before taking money out.

## Ways to reduce the tax

**Qualified charitable distributions (QCDs).** If you’re 70½ or older, you can give money directly from your IRA to a qualified charity, up to an annual limit. A QCD isn’t taxable income and can count toward your RMD. The money must go straight from the IRA to the charity.

**Withholding.** You can have federal tax withheld from an RMD, which counts as paid evenly through the year and can replace estimated payments.

**Planning before RMDs begin.** Roth conversions or withdrawals in lower-income years before RMDs start can reduce future RMDs.

## Ohio

Ohio taxes retirement account withdrawals as income on the state return, though it offers some credits for retirement income. Ohio cities generally don’t tax retirement distributions.
