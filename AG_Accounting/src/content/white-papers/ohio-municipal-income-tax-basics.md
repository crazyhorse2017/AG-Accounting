## Why Ohio is different

Most states have a state income tax. Ohio has one too, but Ohio is also one of the few states where most cities and villages charge their own income tax. If you live or work in Ohio, you may file three kinds of income tax returns: federal, state, and municipal. Some people also file a school district income tax return.

This guide explains how the municipal piece works. Rules and rates vary from city to city, so always confirm the details for the cities that apply to you.

## Who collects city income tax

Ohio cities collect their income tax in one of three ways:

- **RITA, the Regional Income Tax Agency,** collects tax for many member municipalities around the state.
- **CCA, the Central Collection Agency,** collects tax for Cleveland and other member municipalities.
- Some cities **collect their own tax** through a city tax department.

The agency that serves your city is where you file your municipal return and make payments. Your city’s website or the RITA and CCA websites will tell you which applies.

## What is taxed

Municipal income tax generally applies to **earned income**:

- **Wages, salaries, and bonuses**
- **Net profit from a business or self-employment**, including rental activity in many cities

Many types of income are generally not taxed by Ohio cities, such as Social Security benefits, interest and dividends. Treatment of other items, including some retirement income, can vary, so check your city’s rules.

## Where you work and where you live

You can owe tax to two cities: the city where you **work** and the city where you **live**.

1. Your employer usually **withholds** tax for the city where you work.
2. Your **home city** may also tax your income, but most give a **credit** for tax you paid to the city where you work.
3. Credits differ. Some cities give full credit up to their own rate; others give a partial credit or none. When the credit is less than your home city’s tax, you owe the difference to your home city.

**Example:** you live in a city with a 2.5% tax that gives full credit, and work in a city with a 2% tax. Your employer withholds 2% for the work city. Your home city credits that 2%, and you owe the remaining 0.5% to your home city, unless your employer also withholds for your home city as a courtesy.

## Working in several cities

Ohio law has rules for people who work briefly in other cities. In general, an employer withholds for the city where you principally work, and short periods of work in another city during the year usually don’t require withholding there. Employees who travel for work or work from home should ask their employer which city is being withheld for, because remote and hybrid work can change the answer.

## Self-employed people and landlords

If you have business or rental income, no one withholds city tax for you. You usually file a municipal return reporting your net profit and make **estimated payments** during the year if you’ll owe tax. Businesses that operate in several cities may need to divide their profit among them.

## Filing deadlines

Municipal returns for individuals are generally due on the same day as your federal return, usually **April 15**. Many cities accept extensions, often based on your federal extension, but payments are still due by the original deadline.

## Common problems to avoid

- **Assuming your employer withheld enough.** If your employer withholds only for your work city, you may still owe your home city.
- **Moving mid-year.** You may need to file with two home cities for the same year.
- **Forgetting side income.** Business, gig, and rental profit are often taxable by your city even when your wages are covered by withholding.
- **Ignoring letters.** Cities and collection agencies send notices for missing returns, even when no tax is due.

## Where to get help

Your W-2 shows local wages and tax withheld in boxes 18 through 20. Keep your W-2s and any city notices together, and bring them to your tax appointment. AG Accounting can help you sort out which cities you owe and file the right returns.
