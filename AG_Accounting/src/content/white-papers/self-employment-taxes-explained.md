## Who is self-employed

You are generally self-employed if you carry on a trade or business as a sole proprietor or independent contractor, are a member of a partnership that carries on a trade or business, or are otherwise in business for yourself, including part-time. Freelancers, consultants, contractors, rideshare and delivery drivers, and many small business owners fall into this group.

Self-employed people pay two kinds of federal tax on their business profit: **income tax**, like everyone else, and **self-employment tax**, which funds Social Security and Medicare.

## What self-employment tax is

Employees and their employers split Social Security and Medicare taxes. Each pays 7.65% of wages. When you work for yourself, you are both the employee and the employer, so you pay both halves: **15.3%**.

- **12.4% for Social Security**, on earnings up to the yearly wage base. For 2026, the wage base is **$184,500**.
- **2.9% for Medicare**, on all earnings, with no cap.
- An **Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9%** applies to combined wages and self-employment earnings above $200,000 for single filers ($250,000 for joint filers, $125,000 for married filing separately).

## How it’s calculated

Self-employment tax isn’t figured on your full profit. You first multiply your net profit by **92.35%**. That step gives you the same treatment as an employee, whose employer’s share isn’t counted as wages.

**Example:** your Schedule C shows a net profit of $50,000.

1. $50,000 × 92.35% = $46,175 of net earnings from self-employment
2. $46,175 × 15.3% = **$7,064.78** of self-employment tax

If your net earnings from self-employment are less than **$400**, you generally don’t owe self-employment tax. If you also have a W-2 job, your wages count toward the Social Security wage base first, which can lower the Social Security part of your self-employment tax.

## The deduction for half

You can deduct **half of your self-employment tax** when figuring your adjusted gross income. In the example above, that is about $3,532. This deduction lowers your income tax, but not the self-employment tax itself.

## Paying during the year

No one withholds tax from self-employment income, so you are expected to pay as you go with **quarterly estimated tax payments**, usually due April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15.

You can generally avoid an underpayment penalty by paying in, through estimates and any withholding, at least the smaller of:

- **90%** of this year’s total tax, or
- **100%** of last year’s total tax (**110%** if last year’s adjusted gross income was over $150,000).

Your estimates should cover both income tax and self-employment tax. Ohio and most Ohio cities also expect estimated payments when you’ll owe them tax.

## Lowering the tax you owe

Self-employment tax is based on **profit**, so every legitimate business expense you deduct lowers both your income tax and your self-employment tax. Common examples include:

- Supplies, equipment and software used in the business
- Business use of a vehicle (standard mileage rate or actual expenses, with a mileage log)
- The business share of phone and internet costs
- Advertising, insurance, professional fees and licenses
- A home office used regularly and only for business. The simplified method allows **$5 per square foot**, up to **300 square feet**.

Some benefits are reported elsewhere on your return. For example, self-employed health insurance and contributions to retirement plans such as a SEP IRA or solo 401(k) can lower income tax, though not self-employment tax.

## Records that protect your deductions

The IRS can ask you to prove every income figure and deduction on your return. Keep:

- Invoices and records of every payment you receive, including cash
- Receipts, bank and card statements for expenses
- A mileage log showing date, destination, purpose and miles
- Records of equipment purchases and when items were placed in service

Keep these for at least three years after you file, and longer for asset records.

## Common mistakes to avoid

1. **Not setting money aside.** A separate savings account for taxes, funded from every payment you receive, prevents a large April bill.
2. **Mixing personal and business money.** A separate account makes records clear and deductions easier to support.
3. **Forgetting city tax.** Most Ohio cities tax self-employment income, often through RITA or CCA.
4. **Missing estimated payments.** Penalties are figured like interest and keep growing until you pay.
5. **Claiming personal costs as business expenses.** Commuting, personal meals and family use of a vehicle aren’t deductible.
