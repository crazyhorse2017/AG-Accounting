## Two ways to save on taxes

Every retirement account gives you a tax break, either now or later.

- **Traditional** accounts: contributions are pre-tax or deductible, so they lower your taxable income today. Withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income.
- **Roth** accounts: contributions are made with after-tax money. Qualified withdrawals, including all the growth, are tax-free.

If you expect a lower tax rate in retirement than today, traditional accounts tend to come out ahead. If you expect a higher rate, Roth tends to win. Many people use both.

## Workplace plans

**401(k), 403(b), governmental 457, and the federal Thrift Savings Plan**

- 2026 employee contribution limit: **$24,500**
- Catch-up if age 50 or older: **$8,000**
- Higher catch-up at ages 60 through 63: **$11,250**
- Total contributions from you and your employer: up to **$72,000**, not counting catch-ups
- Many plans offer a Roth option.

Contributions come out of your paycheck, which makes saving automatic. Employer matching contributions are one of the best returns available, so contributing at least enough to get the full match is usually a priority.

**SIMPLE IRA** (for small employers)

- 2026 employee limit: **$17,000**
- Catch-up at 50 or older: **$4,000**; at 60 through 63: **$5,250**
- Employers must make either matching or fixed contributions.

## Individual Retirement Arrangements (IRAs)

- 2026 limit: **$7,500**, or **$8,600** if age 50 or older. This limit is shared across all of your traditional and Roth IRAs.
- You need earned income, though a spouse without income can contribute based on the working spouse’s income.
- You can contribute for 2026 until the filing deadline in April 2027.

**Traditional IRA:** contributions may be fully or partly deductible. The deduction can be limited if you or your spouse are covered by a workplace plan and your income is above certain levels.

**Roth IRA:** contributions aren’t deductible and are allowed only below certain income levels. Qualified withdrawals are tax-free, and there are no required minimum distributions for the original owner.

## Plans for the self-employed

Self-employed people can set up their own plans, often with high limits:

- **SEP IRA:** employer contributions based on a percentage of net self-employment earnings; simple to set up and run.
- **Solo 401(k):** for owners with no employees other than a spouse; allows both employee deferrals and employer contributions.
- **SIMPLE IRA:** also available to self-employed people.

The right choice depends on your income, whether you have employees, and how much you want to save. Publication 560 covers the details.

## Taking money out

**Early withdrawals.** Withdrawals before age 59½ are generally subject to a **10% additional tax** on top of regular income tax. Exceptions include certain disability, medical, and separation-from-service situations.

**Rollovers.** Moving money directly between retirement accounts avoids tax and withholding. If a distribution is paid to you, you generally have 60 days to roll it over.

**Required minimum distributions (RMDs).** Traditional accounts require yearly minimum withdrawals starting at age **73**, or **75** for people born in 1960 or later. Your first RMD can wait until April 1 of the following year, but then two are due in that year. Roth IRAs have no RMDs for the original owner.

**Taxes on withdrawals.** Traditional withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, and they can make more of your Social Security benefits taxable. Planning withdrawals over several years can help manage your tax bracket.

## Putting it together

A common approach:

1. Contribute to a workplace plan at least up to the employer match.
2. Add an IRA, traditional or Roth depending on your tax situation.
3. Increase workplace plan contributions with any additional savings.
4. If you’re self-employed, consider a SEP IRA or solo 401(k).

Your best plan depends on your income, tax rates now and later, and your goals. AG Accounting can help you compare your options and plan withdrawals when the time comes.
