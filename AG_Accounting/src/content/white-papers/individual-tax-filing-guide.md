## Why preparation matters

Most tax problems start long before a return is filed: a missing form, a forgotten account, or a life change no one mentioned. A little preparation makes your return more accurate, gets any refund to you faster, and lowers the chance of a letter from the IRS later.

This guide walks through the filing process for a typical individual or family in Ohio. It covers your federal return, your Ohio return, and your city income tax return.

## Step 1: Confirm your filing status

Your filing status affects your tax rates and standard deduction. There are five:

1. **Single**
2. **Married filing jointly:** spouses combine income and deductions on one return. Both are responsible for the tax.
3. **Married filing separately:** each spouse files alone. This often means higher tax but can make sense in some situations.
4. **Head of household:** for unmarried people who pay more than half the cost of keeping up a home for a qualifying person.
5. **Qualifying surviving spouse:** for up to two years after a spouse’s death, for someone with a dependent child.

Your marital status on December 31 generally decides which statuses you can use for the whole year.

## Step 2: Know your standard deduction

Most people take the standard deduction instead of itemizing. For 2026 it is:

- **$16,100** for single filers and married people filing separately
- **$24,150** for heads of household
- **$32,200** for married couples filing jointly

People 65 or older, or blind, add **$2,050** if unmarried or **$1,650** per qualifying spouse if married. From 2025 through 2028, people 65 and older may also qualify for an additional **$6,000** deduction each, which phases out at higher incomes.

You might itemize instead if your mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charitable gifts and large medical expenses add up to more than your standard deduction.

## Step 3: Gather your income documents

Expect most forms to arrive by early February. Watch your mail and your online accounts for:

- **W-2:** wages from each employer. Boxes 18 through 20 show local wages and city tax withheld.
- **1099-NEC and 1099-K:** contract, freelance and gig income.
- **1099-INT and 1099-DIV:** interest and dividends.
- **1099-B:** sales of stocks, funds and other investments.
- **1099-R:** pensions, annuities and retirement account withdrawals.
- **SSA-1099:** Social Security benefits.
- **1099-G:** unemployment compensation and state tax refunds.
- **Schedule K-1:** income from partnerships, S corporations, estates and trusts.

Income is taxable even if you don’t receive a form for it, so also gather your own records of cash income, rental income, and business income and expenses.

## Step 4: Gather deduction and credit records

- **Form 1098:** mortgage interest
- Property tax statements
- Charitable gift receipts (a written acknowledgment is required for any single gift of $250 or more)
- Large medical and dental expenses
- Child care costs, with the provider’s name, address and tax ID
- **Form 1098-T** for college tuition and **1098-E** for student loan interest
- Retirement and HSA contribution records
- **Form 1095-A** if you had Marketplace health insurance

## Step 5: Gather records of tax you already paid

Bring the dates and amounts of any federal, Ohio, or city estimated tax payments, and any payment you made with an extension request.

## Step 6: Review personal information

Check that names and Social Security numbers match the cards for everyone on the return. A mismatch is one of the most common reasons returns are rejected. If you received an **Identity Protection PIN** from the IRS, bring this year’s number.

## Step 7: Understand your Ohio and city returns

Ohio taxes income on its own return, the IT 1040. For 2026, Ohio taxes nonbusiness income above $26,050 at a flat 2.75%. Some school districts also have an income tax, filed on the SD 100.

Most Ohio cities and villages have their own income tax. Depending on where you live, it may be collected by your city, by the Regional Income Tax Agency (RITA), or by the Central Collection Agency (CCA). If you work in one city and live in another, you may owe tax to both, with a credit that depends on your home city’s rules.

## Step 8: File on time, or extend

Individual returns are generally due **April 15**. If you need more time, Form 4868 gives an automatic six-month extension to file. An extension does **not** extend the time to pay: estimate and pay any tax owed by the April deadline to avoid penalties and interest.

## Step 9: Get your refund faster

Filing electronically and choosing direct deposit is the fastest way to get a refund. The IRS issues most refunds for e-filed returns in less than 21 days. You can check the status with the IRS “Where’s My Refund?” tool and with Ohio’s refund status page.

## Step 10: Keep your records

Keep a copy of your return and supporting documents for at least three years. Keep records about property, such as a home or investments, until three years after you report its sale. Some situations call for keeping records longer.

## Quick checklist

- Photo ID and Social Security cards for everyone on the return
- Last year’s federal, Ohio and city returns
- All W-2, 1099, SSA-1099 and K-1 forms
- Records of other income
- Mortgage, property tax, charity, medical, child care and education records
- Records of estimated payments made
- Any letters from the IRS, Ohio, RITA or CCA
- Your Identity Protection PIN, if you have one
